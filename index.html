An independent submission by Andre Nwofor for the Carepatron AI Growth Marketer internship.

Almost half of new practices never come back after their first day.

I analysed 518 trial practices from Carepatron's sample data. Here's what goes with paying, a journey to get more practices there, and a free tool built for one of those moments.

01 · Read the data

Paying goes with two things: coming back after day 0, and real client work.

Of 552 trial workspaces that signed up in July–August 2026, 518 were real practices, and 64 of them paid within 30 days: 12.4% (95% interval 9.8–15.5%). The data is fictional, supplied by Carepatron for the challenge.

1Clean first

34 of the 552 sign-ups weren't real practices, so they came out first.

25 were phishing or spam accounts and 9 were internal QA accounts. The spam accounts impersonate brands with digit swaps, never touched a clinical feature, went quiet after a median of about 63 hours, and none paid.

Left in, they made importing look like something non-payers do. They produced 88% of all client imports and 85% of portal invites. It looks like abuse of the client portal to send phishing invites, worth flagging to trust and safety.

Then one rule for what goes with paying: only events from a practice's first 14 days, and before it paid, count. 86% of paying practices' billing-page views came after they paid, so later events describe customers, not trials.

High These accounts are spam or QA and must be removed

2The day-0 cliff

45% of trials (235 of 518) never came back after day 0. None of them paid.

They did try the product: a median of 3 actions, mostly appointments, notes, templates and hand-added clients. Only 1 of 235 used the scribe, took an online booking or sent an invoice.

And they can't be told apart early. At the end of day 0, leavers look like practices that come back later: about 3 actions, about 100 minutes, and no one has used the scribe yet.

High A large group with a zero conversion rate

3Coming back early

Practices that came back on days 1–3 paid at 30%, against 5%.

Among the 505 practices that hadn't paid by day 3, the 110 that came back on days 1–3 paid at 30.0%; the 395 that didn't, at 4.6%. Two-thirds of those later payers (33 of 51) had come back in that window.

It's partly circular: keen practices both come back and pay. But it sets the clock for a journey, because the typical practice first comes back after about 2.6 days.

High that the link exists; partly circular

4Real work vs trying it out

Three actions still go with paying once activity is allowed for: the AI scribe, online bookings and invoices.

33.9% of practices that took an online booking paid, against 9.7% of those that didn't. Invoices: 32.1% against 10.0%. The AI scribe: 21.8% against 7.4%. Taken one at a time, and allowing for how active a practice was, each goes with about 2.4–2.8 times the odds of paying.

What they share is real client work: a real session written up, a real client booking in, a real bill going out. Notes, appointments and templates, done by 68–75% of trials, show no link to paying once activity is allowed for.

Medium Holds in every check, but intervals are wide and it's borderline after multiple testing

5They add up

The more of the three a practice did, the higher its paid rate.

None: 7.3%. One: 12.7%. Two: 27.3%. All three: 47.4%, though only 19 practices did all three.

Practices doing any of the three paid at 21.4%, about three times the rate of those doing none (7.3%).

Medium Wide intervals, especially for all three

6What this doesn't explain

The pattern has limits, and none of it proves cause.

All of this is correlation, not cause. Practices with real clients can book and invoice; ones without can't, so these actions partly mark practices that were always going to pay. Only a holdout test shows how much a journey can change.

How I checked

The three actions show a clear gap in July and August separately: for example, scribe users paid at 19.8% vs 8.7% in July, and 23.7% vs 5.8% in August.

With duplicate sign-ups collapsed, or the 7 practices that paid within 24 hours removed, the odds ratios for the three stay at 2.6–3.3 (all p < 0.03).

Rates come with 95% Wilson intervals. "Allowing for activity" means a logistic regression that also includes how many distinct days a practice was active.

02 · Design one journey

One entry point. Every step after it waits for what the practice does.

Give every new practice that hasn't come back a reason to return by day 4, and make that reason a piece of real client work.

Entry · 24 hours after sign-upHasn't come back
HoldoutStandard onboarding only
Email 1Your next session, written up for you
In-appOne card, not a checklist
Email 3 · day 4A note from a named person
Email 2 · next morningLet clients book themselves
Exit as successBooks online or invoices

Other exits: pays, unsubscribes, spam rule, day 14

  1. Entry · 24 hours after sign-up

    Everyone who hasn't come back

    Hasn't come back, hasn't used the scribe, taken a booking or sent an invoice, hasn't paid, and isn't flagged by the spam rule: about 85% of new trials (438 of 518).

    At the end of day 0, leavers look just like practices that come back later, so the entry doesn't try to pick them out. Half go to a holdout group that gets standard onboarding only.

  2. Email 1 · on entry

    “Your next session, written up for you.”

    Turn on the AI scribe in your next session, or try it on a 2-minute sample recording. Read the email

  3. In-app · when they log back in

    One card, not a checklist

    Scribe not used yet: “Record your next session”. Already used: “Share your booking link”.

  4. Email 2 · the morning after first scribe use

    “Let clients book themselves.”

    Links to Open Slot, the booking-link kit: wording for a directory profile, email signature and bio, plus a printable QR card. See Open Slot

    For practices that used the scribe but haven't had an online booking.

  5. Email 3 · still no return at day 4

    A plain-text note from a named person

    “Is anything stopping you from trying it on one session this week?” Replies go to a real inbox.

  6. Exit · a client books online or an invoice is sent

    Exit as success

    Standard onboarding carries on. Other exits: pays, unsubscribes, trips the spam rule, or reaches day 14.

Why the scribe comes first

  • 179 practices used it (35% of trials), three times as many as booked online or invoiced.
  • It shows early. Among practices not yet paid by day 3, those that had used it by then paid at 29.0%, against 7.1%.
  • No one used it in their first session, which makes it a natural reason to come back.

Why day 4 is the last nudge

  • Practices whose first return came 20 to 96 hours after sign-up paid at 33%. Those that first came back after day 4 paid at 8%, so later emails mostly add noise.
  • The typical practice first comes back after about 2.6 days.

How we'd know it worked

Randomise at entry and compare against the holdout. The findings are correlational, so only a holdout shows how much of the gap the journey actually closes.

Guardrails

Unsubscribes, spam complaints, consent-related support tickets

Baselines set before launch

With a 50/50 holdout, about 1,000 entrants detect a rise of 8 points (80% power, 5% significance) on the primary measure: about 4.6 months at this dataset's volume of about 219 entrants a month. About 3,400 detect 3 points on paid. An 80/20 split would need about 1,600 (7.3 months).

Decision rule. Keep it if the primary measure rises by 5 points or more and its 95% interval excludes zero, with the guardrails flat.

What it leaves out, on purpose

  • Practices that come back but stay shallow. They only get the in-app card here. They're the next journey I'd build.
  • Practices that arrive already decided. 24 of 64 payers paid without the key actions. The journey exits them at payment and doesn't try to speed them up.
  • UK and Canadian practices. Their gap looks like pricing or checkout, which no email fixes.
  • Separate journeys for each feature. One entry point with one holdout keeps the result readable.

03 · Write one message

Written for the ten minutes between clients.

Email 1 from the journey, for a solo therapist reading on their phone between sessions: one idea, one button, about 80 words.

How it lands in the inbox: subject and preview text.

  1. 1

    From a named person, in plain text

    Sent at 10 minutes to the hour in the practice's local time, when a session has just ended. The sign-off is a stand-in; a real send would come from Carepatron's onboarding lead.

  2. 2

    No unsupported claims

    About a third of trials used the scribe, so the email doesn't say “most therapists” do.

  3. 3

    “Get your client's OK”

    Recording a therapy session needs consent, and therapists know it. Saying so first builds trust; skipping it would read as careless.

  4. 4

    “At 9pm”

    Late-night notes are the pain a solo therapist feels, so that's the benefit to lead with, not “AI”.

  5. 5

    The sample recording

    It removes the “no session today” excuse, so anyone can see the result within minutes.

  6. 6

    One action

    The button opens the scribe ready to record, not a dashboard.

04 · Ship something

Only 11% of trials got an online booking in their first two weeks. Open Slot puts the booking link where new clients look.

A client booking online is one of the three moments that go with paying, yet only 11% of trials (56 of 518) got one in their first 14 days.

Practices that had one paid at 33.9%, against 9.7%. A booking page only brings in bookings if clients can find the link.

The idea

Open Slot is a free tool that turns a practitioner's booking link into ready-to-paste wording for every place a new client might find them, plus a printable QR card.

  • Free
  • No sign-up
  • Nothing stored
  1. 1

    Enter your details and booking link

    Name, practice, who you work with, sessions and fees.

  2. 2

    Get your kit

    Directory profile, enquiry reply, email signature, bio, voicemail, out-of-office and a printable card.

  3. 3

    Copy, paste, print

    Every output has a copy button. The card is business-card size, ready to print.

05 · Tell us about the AI

AI did the heavy lifting. Checking it did the important work.

I used Claude for most of the hands-on work: the analysis code, first drafts, charts and the tool. I checked its work two ways, with prompts built to break its findings and by hand. The checking is where most of the value came from.

Tools

Claude (Opus 5.5, in the Claude app)
Planning, the data analysis (it wrote and ran Python: pandas, SciPy, statsmodels), first drafts of the findings, journey and email, and building the tool.
Claude Docs
The findings doc and its charts.
Claude artifacts
Hosting the first version of Open Slot.
Claude Code
Building this site.
Custom skills
Reusable instructions I set up for Claude.

Every number in the findings comes from code run on the supplied CSVs, not from the model's memory, so each one can be re-run and checked.

How I prompted and checked

  1. 1Structured prompts

    Task, context, files, then a plan

    Each request set out the task, the context and the files, and asked for a plan before any analysis or building, so I could redirect early rather than fix a finished product.

  2. 2Probing prompts

    Built to break the findings, not polish them

    • “Play devil's advocate: try to prove yourself wrong, or validate the finding by being unable to.”
    • “Are these spam accounts really spam?”
    • “Is there a better option for the tool?”

  3. 3Manual validation

    Checked by hand

    I reviewed the accounts it flagged, checked that figures matched across the findings, journey and charts, and checked product claims against Carepatron's own pages.

Where it got something wrong

The first pass found a strong import signal and recommended building a tool around it. Both the number and the recommendation were wrong.

  1. 1First pass

    Importers paid at 36% vs 7%, so: build a client-list converter

    It counted imports across all 30 days, including after practices had paid.

  2. 2Re-run on the right window

    First 14 days, before paying: 28% vs 10%

    With the scribe, online bookings and invoices counted, importing showed no link of its own (odds ratio 0.98, 95% interval 0.39–2.5).

  3. 3Checked against the product

    Carepatron already has a built-in “Import from another platform” option

    It handles SimplePractice exports and spreadsheets. Source: Carepatron help centre

    So the tool became Open Slot instead, aimed at online bookings: one of the three actions that held up in every check.

How I noticed. Before building anything, I asked the AI to play devil's advocate. Re-running each signal with the stated rules and controls exposed the import result; checking Carepatron's own help pages exposed the duplicate. Without that step I'd have built a weaker copy of an existing feature, aimed at a lever the data doesn't support.

Smaller catches

  • The spam filter missed some

    Filtering by email domain caught 21 of the 25 spam accounts. The same devil's-advocate pass found 4 more on a third throwaway domain. A behaviour rule (no clinical activity and 10+ portal invites) caught all 25.

  • The first journey trigger couldn't single out leavers

    It entered practices with no activity 20 hours after sign-up, aimed at the 45% who leave on day 0. It would have caught 86% of trials, because leavers and later returners look identical at the end of day 0. So the journey stopped trying to pick out leavers: it takes in everyone who hasn't come back, with a holdout, and every step after that waits for what the practice does.

  • The tool had two small bugs

    A new service defaulted to 50 minutes, so a “15-minute intro call” read “50 minutes”, and a zero fee printed “$0” instead of “free”. Testing with awkward inputs caught both before I shared it.

What I took from it

  1. The AI is fastest exactly where it's riskiest. It produced a confident number, and a sensible-sounding plan built on it, within minutes. The errors sat underneath, in definitions: which time window, which accounts count, before or after payment.

  2. The most useful prompt asked it to disprove its own work. Every catch above came from questioning an answer. Next time I'd build that step in from the start, and agree the definitions before looking at any results.

  3. Checking against the real world matters as much as checking the maths. The duplicate-feature problem wasn't in the data at all.
